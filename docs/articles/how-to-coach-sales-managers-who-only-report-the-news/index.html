How to Coach Sales Managers Who Only Report the News

By Greg Grand · August 13, 2026

The Short AnswerTo coach sales managers to coach reps, establish a clear, documented coaching process focusing on specific, measurable behaviors. Equip managers with structured tools like the Three-Bucket Forecast for pipeline analysis and regular one-on-ones with defined agendas. Demand managers bring solutions and insights, not just status updates, making them accountable for rep development and skill improvement.

Key Takeaways

  • Managers who only report the news are not coaching, and that will kill your growth.
  • Coaching managers means giving them a concrete system to follow, not just an expectation.
  • The Three-Bucket Forecast provides a practical framework for managers to diagnose deal health.
  • Your role as a leader is to coach the coach, modeling the behavior you want to see.
  • Build accountability into your coaching rhythm with managers, demanding insights and action plans.

Why do my sales managers only report the news?

Here's the reality: most sales managers didn't ask for the job. They were top-performing reps, promoted for individual heroics, not for their ability to build and lead a team. Suddenly, they're responsible for other people's numbers, but they have no map for how to get there. They revert to what they know: asking "what's new?" and then summarizing the answers for you.

I've walked into dozens of sales organizations where this is the norm. The CEO tells me their managers aren't coaching, but they can't articulate what coaching even means. The managers are effectively glorified reporting clerks, pushing status updates up the chain without actually influencing behavior or outcomes on the front lines. This isn't sales leadership; it is just a game of telephone.

This creates a vicious cycle. Reps don't get the guidance they need to improve, so their performance stagnates. Managers get frustrated, feeling like they're babysitting instead of leading. And you, the founder or CEO, end up running the entire sales operation from three layers up, constantly firefighting instead of directing strategy. You can't scale chaos like that.

What's missing when managers just report what happened?

When managers only report the news, they miss the critical "why" behind the numbers. They tell you a deal slipped, but they can't explain the true obstacles or the rep's specific missteps. They recount pipeline activity but offer no insight into its quality or the rep's underlying selling skills. This approach means you have no visibility into the actual health of your sales engine.

True coaching goes deeper. It's about dissecting sales situations, identifying patterns, and prescribing specific actions to improve future outcomes. It means moving beyond a "how was your week?" conversation to a structured analysis of process adherence, buyer engagement, and skill development. If it lives in one rep's head, it isn't a process your manager can coach to.

The biggest gap is often the lack of a consistent, teachable sales process itself. Managers can't coach what isn't defined. They also lack the framework to diagnose issues effectively. They have no system for breaking down a stalled deal or a poor prospecting effort into its component parts, making it impossible to give targeted, impactful advice. This leads to general encouragement instead of concrete direction.

How do I equip my sales managers to actually coach?

You equip your sales managers to coach reps by giving them a system and then coaching them on how to use it. You can't just tell someone to "coach better" any more than you can tell a rep to "sell more." Start with process, not tools. This means defining what good looks like at every stage of your sales cycle and then giving your managers the tools to measure against it.

A critical first step is making sure your managers truly understand your sales process. Not just the stages, but the specific activities, milestones, and buyer commitments required at each step. This foundational knowledge is what allows them to spot deviations or weaknesses in a rep's approach. Without this shared understanding, their coaching will be inconsistent and ineffective.

Next, give them a structured way to run their one-on-one coaching sessions. These meetings shouldn't be about status updates. They are about development. I recommend a simple agenda framework that managers can follow:

  • Review agreed-upon actions from last meeting: Did the rep do what they said they would? Why or why not?
  • Pipeline review (using a diagnostic tool): We'll cover this next, but this isn't just a list of deals.
  • Skill development focus: What one specific skill or behavior will the rep work on this week? Role-play this skill.
  • Forecast review: Understand the qualitative and quantitative picture.
  • Agree on actions for next week: What will the rep accomplish before the next check-in?

This agenda shifts the focus from reporting to problem-solving and skill building. It gives the manager a blueprint for engagement, moving them from passive listener to active developer.

What specific tools can my sales managers use for coaching?

One of the most powerful tools you can give your sales managers is a structured forecasting methodology that forces qualitative insight, not just quantitative reporting. I teach the Three-Bucket Forecast because it drills down into the health of each deal and the rep's confidence level. It moves managers beyond "I think it'll close" to "I know why it will close, or I know what needs to happen."

For every deal a rep is working, your manager should ask these three questions, categorizing deals into buckets:

  • Commit: "Would you bet your job on this closing this month?" This isn't about hope; it's about firm buyer commitment and verified next steps.
  • Best Case: "Do you have a reason beyond hope to believe this closes?" Here, you're looking for legitimate progress and buyer engagement, but perhaps a missing piece or two.
  • Pipeline: "Does this deal have a next step with a date?" If it doesn't, it is not a real opportunity; it is an optimistic fiction.

This simple framework provides managers with a diagnostic lens. When a deal moves from Best Case to Commit, the manager can ask, "What specific actions did you take to move it?" If a deal stays in Pipeline too long, they can probe, "Why isn't there a next step? What can we do to create one, or should we disqualify?" This helps managers coach reps on pipeline management, qualification, and closing skills.

You also need your managers to understand the Three-Layer ICP for targeted prospecting and qualification coaching. It goes beyond firmographics, incorporating behavioral triggers and specific events that signal a true need for your solution. When a rep struggles with lead quality, the manager can coach them back to this detailed ICP.

How do I coach the sales manager to coach using these tools?

You can't expect your managers to adopt a new coaching framework if you don't model it for them. You are the coach of the coach. Your regular one-on-ones with your sales managers should mirror the structure and depth you expect them to have with their reps. Demand insights, not just reports. Push them on the "why" behind their team's performance.

In your meetings with managers, use the same diagnostic questions. When a manager presents a forecast, ask them, "For the deals in your team's Commit bucket, would you bet your job on them closing?" Push them to explain why deals are in Best Case and what specific actions their reps are taking to move them. This isn't micromanagement; it's holding them accountable for the quality of their team's pipeline and their coaching efforts.

Review specific rep situations with your managers. Have them bring you a challenging rep scenario and ask them, "How did you coach them through this? What specific advice did you give? What was the outcome?" This provides an opportunity for you to coach them directly on their coaching technique, identifying gaps and suggesting alternative approaches. Remember,

AI is a multiplier, not a replacement for this human coaching layer. AI amplifies whatever system you have. If you have chaos, AI amplifies chaos.

What does accountability look like for a coaching sales manager?

Accountability for a coaching sales manager moves beyond just hitting the team number. It extends to the health of the pipeline, the development of their reps' skills, and their active participation in the sales process. You aren't just holding them responsible for outcomes; you're holding them responsible for the inputs and the growth trajectory of their team members.

You need to implement a consistent coaching rhythm. This means mandatory, structured one-on-ones, pipeline reviews, and joint calls. For example, a manager should commit to two joint calls per rep per month. The manager isn't there to take over the call; they are there to observe, diagnose, and provide feedback immediately after. This active participation provides real-world coaching opportunities they can't get from just reviewing CRM notes.

Build key performance indicators around coaching activities, not just sales numbers. Track the frequency and quality of coaching sessions. Monitor rep improvement on specific skills identified during coaching. This shifts the focus from simply hitting a number to building a sustainable, high-performing sales engine.

Your calendar is your operating plan.
If coaching isn't on it consistently, it isn't happening.

You need to be direct. If a manager consistently brings you only "news reports" instead of insights and coaching plans, call it out. Ask them what their plan is to address the specific rep challenge you discussed last week. If they don't have one, help them build one, then hold them to it. This isn't about being harsh; it's about holding everyone to the professional standards required for growth.

The days of passive sales management are over. Your sales managers are either actively developing your team and driving growth, or they're a bottleneck preventing it. If you want to scale your revenue, you must invest in teaching your managers how to coach reps. Give them the systems, the tools, and the consistent coaching they need.

Training your managers is a non-negotiable investment. It pays dividends in retention, performance, and predictable growth. Are your managers building a team or just reporting the weather?

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the primary reason sales managers fail at coaching?

Sales managers often fail at coaching because they lack a defined process and specific tools for effective development. Promoted from top sales reps, they haven't been taught how to diagnose skill gaps or structure coaching conversations, leading them to simply report results instead of guiding improvement.

How does the Three-Bucket Forecast help managers coach reps?

The Three-Bucket Forecast forces managers to ask specific questions about deal health and rep confidence: "Would you bet your job on this closing?" (Commit), "Do you have a reason beyond hope?" (Best Case), and "Does this deal have a next step with a date?" (Pipeline). This diagnostic approach enables targeted coaching on qualification and deal progression.

How can I ensure my sales managers are truly holding coaching sessions?

Implement a mandatory, structured coaching rhythm with clear agendas for one-on-ones, pipeline reviews, and joint calls. Track attendance and the quality of discussion, not just completion. As the leader, model the behavior you expect and hold your managers accountable for both the process and the development outcomes.

What should I do if a sales manager only brings me status updates?

If a sales manager only brings status updates, directly address the expectation that they bring insights and coaching plans. Ask them "why" certain situations occurred and "what specific actions" they took with their rep. Coach them on *how* to diagnose and plan for improvement, then hold them accountable to that new standard.
